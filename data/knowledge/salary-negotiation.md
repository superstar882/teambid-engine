# Salary negotiation

Group: Offers · Related vault file: `Job/Playbook/Negotiation.md` · 6 sources, 11 findings

## Findings (strongest sources first)

- Employers with 15 or more employees must include a pay scale in job postings, and if they use a third party to post the job, that third party must include the pay scale too. **15+ employees; pay scale = good-faith salary or hourly wage range the employer reasonably expects to pay** (California Legislative Information, n.d.; high)
  - Takeaway: An early-stage startup may be under 15 people and exempt from posting ranges, but any employer must give the pay scale on reasonable request.
- In a natural field experiment with nearly 2,500 job seekers, men negotiated more than women when the posting was silent on negotiability. **nearly 2,500 job-seekers randomized** (National Bureau of Economic Research (published in Management Science, Vol. 61 No. 9, 2015), 2012-11; high)
- California employers may not seek an applicant's salary history, orally or in writing, personally or through an agent, and may not rely on it to set an offer. **"shall not, orally or in writing, personally or through an agent, seek salary history information"** (California Legislative Information, n.d.; high)
- Among those who asked, about two-thirds got more than the initial offer. **28% got what they asked for, 38% got more than offered but less than asked, 35% got only the original offer** (Pew Research Center, 2023-04-05; high)
  - Takeaway: Asking usually improves the offer and rarely costs it; the realistic outcome is a partial bump, not the full ask.
- Only about 3 in 10 U.S. workers asked for higher pay than offered the last time they were hired. **32% of men and 28% of women asked for higher pay (survey of 5,775 U.S. adults)** (Pew Research Center, 2023-04-05; high)
- The most common reasons for not negotiating were satisfaction with the offer or discomfort asking. **39% satisfied, 38% didn't feel comfortable asking** (Pew Research Center, 2023-04-05; high)
  - Takeaway: Discomfort is the main barrier; scripting the ask in advance helps.
- When the job ad explicitly said wages were negotiable, the gender difference in negotiating disappeared and even tended to reverse. **"when we explicitly mention the possibility that wages are negotiable, this difference disappears, and even tends to reverse"** (National Bureau of Economic Research (published in Management Science, Vol. 61 No. 9, 2015), 2012-11; high)
- Naming the lowest number you'll accept caps the offer near that number; less experienced candidates are advised to defer numbers until the company sees their value. **"If you name the lowest number you'll accept, you can be pretty sure the company's not going to exceed it"** (Holloway, 2022-09-12; high)
  - Takeaway: Don't give a floor to the recruiter; give a researched range or defer.
- The report highlights negotiation wins, including a data engineer at Lyft who raised an offer by $78,000. **+$78,000** (Levels.fyi, 2025; low)
  - Takeaway: Anecdotal, but shows data-engineering offers have room to move.
- A Robert Half survey of 2,700+ workers found only 39% tried to negotiate their last job offer. **39% negotiated; ages 18-34: 45%** (SHRM, 2018-02-20; medium)
  - Takeaway: Younger workers negotiate more often than older ones, so negotiating is normal for an early-career hire.
- A 2017 Jobvite study cited in the article found most negotiators got a higher salary. **84% of those who negotiated received higher salaries; one-fifth got 11-20% more** (SHRM, 2018-02-20; low)
  - Takeaway: Supports making a counter, but this is vendor survey data from 2017, so treat the exact rate loosely.
